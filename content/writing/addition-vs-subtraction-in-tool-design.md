“Why does this tool need so much setup just to save me an hour?”

After nearly 20 years of using Revit and architectural software, I’ve had that thought more times than I can count.

A recent Plumbing Fixture Count workflow gave me a very direct example.

Our engineering team developed a comprehensive Revit-based system. It was rigorous, highly automated, and capable of tracking many parameters.

But it also required more setup, more training, more maintenance, and produced a fairly rigid, data-heavy output.

My first reaction was:

“If I spend this much time configuring the tool, how much time am I really saving?”

A plumbing fixture calculation may only happen at a few key points during a project. If a tool saves an hour, but requires significant setup and relearning when the team changes a year later, the automation itself can become another workflow to manage.

So I took almost the opposite approach with the tool I built.

Revit occupancy schedule PDF or screenshot

→ simple web tool

→ automatic fixture calculation

→ editable Excel output

→ architect reviews and adjusts

→ PDF links back into Revit

The goal was never maximum automation.

It was to simplify the workflow to the right level.

Fewer inputs.

Less setup.

Less training.

Less maintenance.

Less friction.

Enough automation to get roughly 80% of the work done quickly, while keeping the remaining 20% easy for an architect to adjust.

Looking at the two approaches side by side, I realized the difference was not only software design.

It was two different design instincts.

One kept asking:

“What else can we add?”

The other kept asking:

“What can we remove without losing what actually matters?”

That may also be one reason architects are well suited to building small tools for our own work.

Architecture trains us to make decisions through subtraction.

We constantly balance program, cost, constructability, user needs, aesthetics, and technical requirements. Good design is often not about adding more. It is about understanding what is essential and removing what is not.

That same judgment applies to software.

The person living inside the workflow every day often knows:

“What information is truly necessary?”

“What is good enough to make the decision?”

“Where is human adjustment faster than more automation?”

Until recently, understanding those things did not mean we could build the tool ourselves.

AI-assisted coding is changing that.

Experienced architects do not need to become software engineers first. If we can clearly explain the problem, the workflow, and what a useful result should look like, AI can help turn that domain knowledge into a working tool surprisingly quickly.

For small, focused tools, the advantage may not come from building more.

It may come from knowing what not to build.

“The best tool is not necessarily the one that automates the most. It is the one that removes the most friction.”
