“Wow, Dad. This is so fun.”

That was my daughter’s first reaction when she played the math game we had been building.

Recently, she started learning multiplication. She loves games, so instead of giving her another worksheet, I wanted to see if I could turn multiplication practice into something she would actually enjoy.

That became WiWi’s Three Little Pigs.

After building Animal Kingdom with her earlier, I had already started to notice something surprising: making a game feels a lot like running an architecture project.

There is an idea, a user, a goal, a lot of small decisions, testing, revisions, and plenty of moments where something sounds good in theory but does not quite work once someone actually uses it.

My role was figuring out how to combine the kinds of games and stories she already likes with the learning goal I had in mind, while bringing some of the ideas from the games she had already helped design into this one.

AI filled in a lot of the gaps I do not naturally have: coding, artwork, debugging, testing, and iteration.

And her “Wow” reaction felt strangely familiar.

I have had the same reaction myself more than once while working with AI.

Sometimes it is a small tool I would not have known how to code.

Sometimes it is a new way to visualize an architectural idea.

Sometimes it is a film, a workflow, or simply an idea that used to stay in the “maybe someday” category.

The exciting part is not the AI itself.

It is watching something that used to feel outside your skill set suddenly become real enough to use, test, and improve.

As architects, we already spend our days understanding users, organizing complex problems, coordinating many moving parts, testing ideas, and refining them.

Once AI starts filling in some of the gaps around those skills, the range of things we can make begins to expand well beyond our normal work.

In this case, it happened to be a math game for my daughter.

She kept playing.

And somewhere in the middle of the pigs, the wolf, the dragon, and building houses, she was practicing exactly the multiplication I had hoped she would learn.

That was the best part.

Her first reaction was “Wow, Dad.”

Mine was pretty similar.
