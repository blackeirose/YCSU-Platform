*What an AI-assisted fire station film taught me about capability, specialization, and the changing economics of architectural work.*

## AI Is Changing How Much Scale You Need

I texted my wife from the plane:

“***I just demolished our house and built a fire station.***”

Our house is fine.

Before flying from Honolulu to San Francisco, I had been shooting drone footage around Mānoa Valley, including our home, while preparing material for a drone presentation.

During the flight, another idea came to me.

What if I used our house as a fictional site, demolished it, showed the construction process, and replaced it with a small fire station?

There was no real project and no plan to tear down our home. I simply wanted to see whether familiar footage could become an architectural story.

A lot of the early discussion and storyboarding happened on my phone. I worked with AI to develop the sequence, identify the shots, refine the transitions, and decide what needed to remain recognizable.

The actual production still involved several tools and many decisions. AI helped develop prompts, generate portions of the video, support editing, and work through sound and music.

I remained responsible for the direction: what to pursue, what to reject, what needed another iteration, and when the result was good enough to show.

Later, during my presentation, I joked that I had demolished our house and built a fire station. The audience laughed, and then I played the video.

What stayed with me was not simply that AI could help make a film.

It was that an idea I would once have placed in the “maybe someday” category had become practical enough to begin immediately.

### The ideas we used to postpone

Architecture is full of ideas that are technically possible but difficult to justify.

A short pursuit film.

A custom visualization.

A better design diagram.

A small internal software tool.

A workflow improvement that would save time—after someone first found the time to build it.

The real barrier is often not whether something can be done. It is whether the people, software, training, coordination, and production effort make sense for the task.

Larger firms have traditionally addressed this through scale. They can support visualization specialists, BIM and computational teams, researchers, software developers, communication teams, and people with deep expertise in narrow areas.

A small firm cannot maintain that entire bench.

An individual architect certainly cannot.

AI does not remove the need for specialists. What it is beginning to change is how much specialization must be permanently available before a team can access a useful portion of those capabilities.

That is a much narrower claim, but I think it matters.

### Accessing a capability without building a department

The fire station video is only the latest example in my own work.

Over the past several months, I have used AI to help create small pyRevit tools, develop a lightweight plumbing fixture calculation workflow, build a simplified project-management tool, participate in repetitive Revit production, and develop a native vehicle family from references and defined requirements.

None of that makes me a software engineer, filmmaker, BIM developer, or visualization specialist.

When deep expertise is required, I would still want someone who truly practices that discipline.

The change is that I may not need a dedicated specialist every time I need a small amount of the capability.

A team does not have to establish a software group before testing one useful internal tool.

It does not need a visualization department before experimenting with one short narrative film.

And an architect does not always have to wait for another group’s availability before exploring a small improvement to the way the work is done.

The threshold for starting has moved.

### The real cost is not the subscription

Comparing an AI subscription with the salary of an employee has never made much sense to me.

A more useful comparison is the total effort required to complete a specific task to an acceptable standard.

That includes software, setup, training, human time, review, corrections, coordination, and rework.

AI does not eliminate those costs.

Sometimes it saves substantial time. Sometimes it produces several unusable attempts before anything works. Sometimes the conventional method is still clearly better.

But lowering the cost of trying changes which ideas survive.

If an experiment requires several people, a new software package, outside support, and a week of coordination, many ideas never begin.

When the first test can happen at a much smaller scale, it becomes easier to discover whether the idea deserves more investment.

For a small practice, that can be significant.

### From one experiment to a reusable method

The fire station project did not end entirely when the video was finished.

The following day, I started organizing parts of the workflow into a basic reusable Skill for the next film.

The AI had not permanently learned my preferences. I was simply preserving what had worked: useful instructions, sequencing, examples, expectations, and lessons from the first attempt.

The cycle became:

**Idea → collaboration and testing → finished work → reusable method → next project → refinement**

The next film will still require judgment and new decisions. But it does not have to begin from zero.

The same pattern has appeared in the small tools I have built. A one-time solution can gradually become part of a more reliable working method.

That may be as important as the first output.

### Why large and small firms may see this differently

A large firm may look at an early AI-generated tool, film, or visualization and reasonably say:

“We already have people who can do this better.”

That may be true.

Large practices still have major advantages: specialist depth, accumulated experience, institutional knowledge, established systems, and the ability to coordinate large multidisciplinary projects.

AI does not erase those strengths.

A small firm may look at the same result and reach a different conclusion:

“We did not have access to this capability before. Now we can at least begin.”

Both perspectives can be valid.

The important change is not that small firms have suddenly become large firms.

It is that some capabilities that once depended heavily on organizational scale are becoming accessible at a smaller scale.

Inside large firms, the opportunity may be different.

If architects, project managers, and designers can prototype narrowly defined tools around the problems they understand best, every small idea does not need to begin as a request to a central technology group.

Specialist teams remain essential. They can focus more of their attention on work that actually requires specialist depth, while project teams test smaller ideas closer to the source of the problem.

The people doing the work gain more ways to act on what they know.

### Experience becomes more usable

The longer I work with AI, the less I think the central skill is knowing which button to press.

The more important skill is recognizing what role AI should play at a particular moment.

Sometimes I need research.

Sometimes I need a second perspective.

Sometimes I want repetitive work handled.

Sometimes I need help writing code.

Sometimes I want to test an idea that would otherwise take too long to justify.

The technology still does not know my project intent unless I can explain the context, constraints, examples, and definition of a useful result.

Experience helps with that.

It helps define what matters, identify unusual results, recognize risk, and determine where simplification is acceptable.

Experience alone is not enough. AI fluency is a separate skill, and younger designers may develop it very quickly.

The powerful combination is domain knowledge plus the ability to collaborate effectively with the technology.

### Judgment remains part of the workflow

I do not accept something simply because AI produced it.

I also do not stop reviewing work simply because another person produced it.

The level of verification should match the consequence of being wrong.

AI can help organize assumptions, compare options, research unfamiliar areas, and prepare better questions for consultants.

It cannot make a structural safety decision and ask the engineer to confirm it later.

It can support an architectural concept film.

That does not make a generated drawing set construction-ready.

The fair comparison is not a perfect human against an imperfect machine.

It is the same task, the same quality requirement, and the total process needed to reach an acceptable result—including review and rework.

### How much scale will an idea require?

The effects will not be uniform across architecture.

Narrow, repeatable, and easily reviewed tasks will change sooner.

Complex civic projects with significant life-safety responsibilities, regulatory risk, consultant coordination, and many interacting systems will continue to rely heavily on experienced teams and specialist judgment.

But the work around the work is already changing:

Research.

Visualization.

Option studies.

Communication.

Small internal tools.

Knowledge capture.

Parts of production.

For a small practice, this can mean testing capabilities that previously were not worth the overhead.

For a large practice, it can mean giving experienced people more ways to turn their knowledge into tools and methods.

For an individual architect, it means an idea that appears somewhere between Honolulu and San Francisco does not always have to wait for a team, a separate budget, and an empty week on the calendar.

Sometimes it is practical enough to begin.
