“What if AI is not only helping us build tools, but actually becoming part of the production workflow?”

Earlier this week, I had close to 30 Revit materials that needed to be developed and assigned for different departments.

The work was useful, but I also had two presentations, meetings, and programming/modeling work that needed my attention. Doing the materials manually could easily have taken a couple of hours.

So I defined the system, color logic, Graphic/Appearance requirements, and boundaries, then gave the task to an AI agent.

It analyzed the departments, developed related color groups, created the materials, and worked through the assignments.

Another experiment went further.

I needed a new SF tiller truck Revit family — not a rendering asset or heavy mesh, but a real production family with native geometry, dimensionable clearances, plan/elevation readability, and turning-radius information.

I gave the AI an existing family as the reference standard, target vehicle photos, dimensions, and requirements.

The assignment was almost identical to what I might give another designer:
“Study this precedent.”

“Understand what information matters.”

“Build the new family to the same standard.”

I didn’t tell it how to model every extrusion. It had to analyze, research, simplify, build, test, and verify.

That is where this starts to feel different from traditional automation.
Automation is still extremely valuable. But automation alone is now becoming the baseline.

The bigger opportunity is when AI combines automation with generation, research, reasoning, context, verification, and judgment within clearly defined boundaries.

At that point, it starts to feel less like another button and more like another type of team player.

That does not make architectural judgment less important.
It makes it more important.

We still need to decide what should be delegated, what should not, what “good” looks like, and when the result is ready to trust.

For me, the goal is not to remove work from architecture.

It is to reclaim more of our professional time for the parts of architecture that actually need an architect.
