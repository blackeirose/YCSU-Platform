Make the Impossible Possible

Lately, I’ve noticed two very different reactions to AI among people around me.

Some are excited—constantly experimenting, learning, and wondering what they can do next.

Others are more cautious. The speed of change creates uncertainty, and uncertainty can easily turn into resistance or fear.

Recently, something simple made me think about this difference from another perspective.

My seven-year-old daughter, Rachel, just started a new AI, Coding & VR class in her after-school program.

After her first class, what struck me most wasn’t what she learned. It was her reaction.

She was excited, curious, and couldn’t wait to go back.

She immediately started talking about what she wanted to try and what we could make next:

“Can we make this?”
“Can we try that?”
“What if we do this?”

Watching her reminded me how differently children can approach new technology.

She carries none of the baggage many of us adults do. She doesn’t first ask whether AI will replace someone, whether this is how things are “supposed” to be done, or whether she should fear something she doesn’t fully understand.

She sees a new tool—and immediately sees possibility.

As adults, especially in a profession as established as architecture, we often react differently.

The unknown can lead to resistance.
Resistance can become fear.
And fear can become a reason to stand still.

But my experience with AI has taught me almost the opposite:

The more we understand, the less there is to fear.

The more we explore, the more possibilities we discover.

AI is advancing at an extraordinary pace. Things that seemed beyond my skill set not long ago are becoming things I can actually explore today.

My question has gradually changed from:

“What can AI do?”

to:

“What can I do now that I couldn’t do before?”

I’ve spent most of my career designing buildings, not writing software.

Yet today, I can build tools for architectural workflows, explore automation, experiment with new ways to communicate design, and turn ideas that once stopped at “I wish we could…” into things I can actually test and build.

AI doesn’t automatically make the impossible possible.

But it dramatically lowers the barrier between imagination and creation.

The next generation will grow up with AI whether we embrace it or not. For Rachel’s generation, working alongside AI may eventually feel as natural as the internet or smartphones feel to us today.

So perhaps the question for our generation—and for architecture—isn’t whether we can resist this change.

What could we discover if we approached the unknown with more curiosity, less fear, and a willingness to learn?

Watching Rachel begin her journey with AI reminded me that sometimes the best response to a powerful new tool is also the simplest:

“Let’s see what we can make.”
